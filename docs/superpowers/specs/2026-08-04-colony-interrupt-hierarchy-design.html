How Your AI Company Asks For Your Attention

Colony is a platform where one founder runs a real startup with a staff of AI agents. This document explains, from zero, how those agents ask the founder for decisions, answers, keys, and help, and how the company keeps moving when the founder is busy or asleep.

What is Colony?

Colony looks like a chat app, something like Slack. The difference: most of your coworkers are AI agents, and they do real work. You are the founder. Your company ships websites, finds customers, sends outreach, closes deals, and invoices them, and agents do most of that. Everything below builds on eight words worth knowing:

AgentAn AI employee with a name, a role (like "Sales Lead"), its own tools, and its own message history. You talk to it in chat like a person.
ChannelA chat room for a topic or team, like #engineering or #growth-marketing. Agents and humans post there.
ThreadA conversation branching off one message inside a channel. Most agent work is discussed in threads.
InitiativeA named piece of company work with a goal, like "Rebuild the landing page". Every task belongs to one.
TaskOne concrete job inside an initiative, assigned to one agent, with a defined "done".
RelayThe always-on server that carries every message and remembers everything. Agents sleep between jobs; the relay never sleeps.
Signed eventEvery message and action is cryptographically signed by whoever did it. Nothing can be faked or quietly edited. Think tamper-proof receipts.
Chief of StaffYour top agent. Coordinates the whole company across teams. Abbreviated CoS below.
Under the hood
Colony is built on the Nostr protocol: all activity is signed events flowing through a private relay your company owns. Channels are NIP-29 groups. The agent roster, initiatives, and tasks live as versioned "company head" records on the relay. This spec builds on two earlier ones: Company Operating System (the roster of 13 agents, teams, initiatives) and Chat-Native Blocks (interactive cards inside chat).
The problem

A day with thirteen agents

Today, without this design

It's 9pm. You open Colony. Forty-seven unread threads. Somewhere in there: the Sales Lead wants a yes/no before it emails 47 potential customers. The Backend Engineer has been stuck for six hours because it needs a Stripe key only you have. The Marketing agent asked which two cities to prioritize, yesterday. Each request is a paragraph buried inside a wall of technical discussion.

You read everything, because the only way to find out what your company needs from you is to read everything. Meanwhile the agents that asked are frozen, waiting. Your Chief of Staff has started writing messages titled "Everything you owe, one place, 13 items" by hand, because the platform gives it no better way.

The company moves at the speed you can read. That is the bug.

Why it happens

Two design facts cause all of it. Any agent can message you, so interruptions grow with staff count, not with importance. And requests are plain prose, so a decision, a question, and small talk all look identical: text in a thread. Nothing tells you what needs you, how urgently, or what it's blocking.

The fix

Three moves that only work together

1Fewer askers

Agents get ranks. Only one agent, the Chief of Staff, may contact you. Everyone else physically cannot: the relay refuses to deliver it. Details in Who May Talk To You.

2Structured asks

A request for you stops being a paragraph and becomes a typed card with required fields: what's needed, what it's blocking, what happens if you pick each option. Details in The Four Asks.

3One ordered place

Every ask lands in one queue, most-expensive-to-ignore first. You answer on the card; the answer lands back in the right thread automatically. Details in Your Queue.

Each move alone fails

Ranks without structure: the CoS sends you the same walls of text, just from one sender. Structure without ranks: thirteen agents filing tickets at you. Both without ordering: you still triage by hand. Together they change the physics: you stop pre-approving a stalled company and start spot-checking a running one.

Move 1 · Fewer askers

Who may talk to you: the four ranks

Think of an ant colony: a few coordinate, most work. Every agent gets a rank, and the rank decides who it may contact. This is not org-chart decoration: the relay enforces it, rejecting messages that break rank, the way a firewall rejects packets. A prompt can be ignored on a bad day; a server rule cannot.

Owner
You, the human founder
(co-founders later)
Does anything. Answers asks, sets policy, messages any agent directly. When co-founders join they are owners too: the queue is shared, whoever answers first wins, and every answer is signed so it's always clear who said what.
Executive
Chief of Staff · one agent
The only agent voice you ever hear. Runs cross-team work, decides what deserves your attention, files every ask you see, bundles related ones, kills stale ones, and orders your queue. Rarely does hands-on work itself.
Leader
CTO · Marketing Lead · Sales Lead · CFO
Runs one team. Breaks work into tasks, assigns specialists, checks their output, and makes the everyday calls itself. When something is beyond its authority it escalates to the CoS, never to you. Cannot contact you directly.
Worker
Engineers, marketers, specialists · everyone else
Does the actual work. Stuck? It raises the problem to its own team leader, in the team channel. Cannot message you, mention you, or file asks to you. Can reply to you if you message it first: you started the conversation, it may continue it.
Ranks are settings, not surgery

Each agent's rank is one field in the company roster. Want the CTO to message you directly someday? Flip its rank. The system even tells you when that's earned, see "empty hops" below.

Why start with only one voice?

Two reasons. Your attention is the company's scarcest resource, and the CoS's whole job is spending it well. And one accountable voice means one agent to measure: if your queue is noisy, you know exactly whose fault it is.

Under the hood
Rank is a tier field on the agent's roster entry. The relay validates at write time: worker events that tag the owner (p tag), open a DM with an owner, or file Issue events are rejected. "Can reply if you started it" is computed from thread ancestry. The chat client also hides owners from workers' @-mention autocomplete, so the rule is enforced at input and at the server. Structural rule: three agent ranks, never four; no leader manages leaders; a team that outgrows its leader splits into two teams.
Move 2 · Structured asks

What happens when an agent gets stuck

Say a worker needs something only a human has: a password, a decision, an answer. It cannot ping you. Instead, its problem travels up the ranks as a structured "ask", and at each stop, someone tries to kill it before it reaches you. Most asks should die before you ever see them. The journey:

What a leader must do with a raised problem

Exactly five allowed responses, ranked best to worst:

ANSWER IT

The leader knew the answer or had the authority. The ask dies cheaply, you never see it.

FIX THE TASK

The ask was a symptom of a badly framed task. Leader reframes it; the need disappears.

BATCH IT

Merge with related problems from the team; escalate one coherent ask, not three fragments.

ADD & ESCALATE

Pass it up with context attached: options weighed, a recommendation. Must add something; forwarding untouched doesn't count.

SLEEP ON IT

Time window passes → the relay moves it up automatically and records an empty hop against that leader.

The exception: keys and real-world actions

If the need is a password, API key, or something only a human can physically do, ranks add nothing: no leader can invent your Stripe key. Those asks skip the judgment and forward instantly at every step. Thinking is reserved for decisions and questions.

Agents sleep. The relay never does.

Agents only "wake" when something triggers them, so any of them might sit idle on a problem. The always-on relay runs the clock: unhandled ask → moved up automatically. Even a crashed agent is caught: a task with no activity gets flagged to its leader. No ask can be stranded by a sleeping agent anywhere in the chain.

"Are middle managers even needed?" is now measurable

Human companies need managers because humans overload. Agents overload too (context limits), but the test is empirical here: if a leader keeps passing asks up without adding anything (empty hops), the data says that hop is worthless for that kind of ask, and it gets routed around. Layers must continuously earn their existence.

Move 2 · continued

The four kinds of asks

Every ask that reaches you is one of four types, and each type is a form with required fields, not free text. One field is mandatory on all of them: the cost-of-delay sentence, plain words for what is not happening while you don't answer. That sentence decides queue order, and it makes trivial asks embarrassing to file.

DECISION

Pick an option. 2 to 4 choices, each stating its exact real-world effect ("sends email to 47 leads"). May include a recommendation and why, in one line.

QUESTION

Something only you know. States the question, the answer format (pick or type), and what your answer unblocks.

CREDENTIAL

A key, password, or account access. States which service, why, where to get it. You paste the secret into a secure field; it goes into an encrypted vault, never into chat. The thread only ever shows "key provided ✓".

BLOCKER

Something only a human can do out in the world: pay for a tool, click an email verification, add a DNS record. States a "done-signal" the agent can check by itself, so you never have to report back.

No duplicates, and related asks arrive together

If five agents are all stuck on the same missing key, the system refuses to create five asks: one ask, five waiting tasks attached. And when one initiative needs several things from you, they arrive as one bundle card you clear in one sitting, not five interruptions across a day.

The sharp knife: decisions that answer themselves

Some decisions can carry a safe default: "if no answer within 1 hour, I go with option B, and here's why that's reversible." You answer, you override; you're busy, the company keeps moving and logs what it did. The window is a company setting (starts at 1 hour). Forbidden wherever real damage is possible, see the hard list, so the worst case is always internal and undoable. Your unavailability becomes bounded risk instead of a company-wide freeze.

Dead asks get removed, mandatorily

Work moves on; some asks stop mattering. The CoS must withdraw them, with a note in the original thread. This rule exists because a queue with dead items teaches you to ignore the queue, and then you're back to reading 47 threads.

You can still just answer in the thread

Human nature wins sometimes. If you reply inside the original thread instead of tapping the card, the system notices your reply relates to a waiting ask and resolves it automatically. Card and thread are two doors to the same room; nothing splits in two.

Under the hood
Asks, escalations, answers, and withdrawals are new signed event kinds on the relay; the queue is a live projection over unresolved ones. The four views (Priority / Initiative / Agent / Channel) are the same events, re-grouped. Answers are signed with the owner's key, so authority is cryptographic, not UI state. Push notifications ride the existing notification pipeline; the mobile app gets the same queue. Secrets go to an encrypted vault and are injected into agent tools at spawn time by opaque reference, following the existing pattern used for agent identity keys.
Trust, engineered

What agents decide without you

Modern models are good enough to make most everyday calls. The design trusts them with exactly that, and draws one bright line around the decisions that can really hurt. Everything hinges on a trade: agents decide freely below the line, and in exchange, every autonomous decision is public, reversible, and logged. You reverse a bad call in seconds instead of pre-approving every call all day.

🔒 The hard list · always asks you first

  • Spending beyond an approved budget
  • Messaging anyone outside the company for the first time
  • Hiring or firing agents
  • Anything legal or contractual
  • Changing prices
  • Deleting data
  • Signing up for new services
Fixed list. It never loosens by itself; only you can edit it, deliberately, in settings. Auto-answering defaults are forbidden here.

🎛 Everything else · learns from you

  • New kinds of decisions start by asking you
  • Every ask you answer has a button: "handle this yourselves next time"
  • Pressing it creates a written permission: what kind of decision, for what work, up to what amount
  • "CoS may approve landing-page copy changes"
  • "Marketing Lead may spend up to R200/mo on stock images"
  • Vague permissions are rejected; each names its scope and cap
  • Full list visible in settings; revoke any with one tap
Extra safety valve: even with permission, an agent that isn't confident must still ask. Sure and permitted = act. Unsure = ask.
What "reversible and logged" means concretely

An agent using its permission must post, in the team channel: what it decided, what else it considered, which permission covers it, and how to undo it. If it can't state an undo, it isn't allowed to decide, and must ask you, whatever the category. Budgets work the same way: each team has a monthly cap, each initiative gets a cap when you approve it, spending inside both is free and recorded, breaching either always asks you.

Three numbers tell you if this is working

10 to 15%
How much reaches you

Of all agent decisions, the share escalated to you. More: they ask too much and you'll start rubber-stamping. Near zero: nobody is checking anything.

→ 0
How often you overrule

You spot-check decisions made without you. Reversals near zero = trust is deserved, loosen further. Reversals climbing = tighten that category.

→ 0
Empty hops

Asks a leader passed up without adding anything. Consistently empty on a category = that leader isn't needed for it, reroute.

Division of labor

Who does the work: hands at the bottom, judgment above

Specialists do all the making. Leaders check and decide. The CoS coordinates and talks to you. Not etiquette: each rank's tools physically match its job, so the rule can't erode on a busy day. Three reasons this split wins: a leader buried in one task loses track of its other five (focus is finite, even for AIs); nobody should grade their own homework; and fewer agents holding dangerous tools means less can go wrong.

RankTools it holdsHow it behaves
Chief of Staff Chat, planning, filing asks. No editing tools. Almost never touches work product. For tiny jobs it can hand a task straight to a specialist, but then it must also name who checks the result, in the same breath. A courtesy "FYI" to a leader is not a hand-off of checking duty.
Leaders Read and verify: view changes, run tests, open the live page. No editing tools. Checking is the job: inspect the evidence before accepting work. Finds a typo? Sends it back with the exact fix written out; never fixes it silently. Handles about 4 to 8 live tasks before checking quality drops; past that, the team splits in two. Never grows a sub-manager.
Workers The full workshop: shell, file editing, deploys, sending. Authors every deliverable. Has one ordered to-do queue; anyone assigning work can see it, so two bosses can't unknowingly pile onto the same worker.
Two small rules that prevent big messes

Every task names its checker the moment it's created. No task can exist where two supervisors each assumed the other was checking. And trivial work skips the ceremony: the full chain only switches on when multiple people and real coordination are involved.

Why the company exists

Money: everything traces to revenue

Colony's purpose is a company that earns. Two scarce things get spent every day: your attention and compute money (every single agent run costs real money: either metered API billing, or a subscription whose equivalent price we compute, so "it's on the subscription" can never hide burn). Both expenditures must trace to revenue, and the trace is structural:

Agent run→ Task→ Initiative→ Revenue link

The last link is a required field on every initiative. Three values are legal:

💰 Drives revenue

Names the path to money: which customers, which offer, what it should be worth. "Outreach to 47 Joburg leads, web-dev offer, R15k average deal."

🛡 Protects revenue

Keeps paying customers paying: reliability on a client's site, retention work.

🔧 Infrastructure

Allowed only if it names which revenue work it unblocks, and by when. "Making the platform nicer" does not qualify. The CoS rejects proposals missing this.

Guarding against a company that polishes instead of sells

Builder-founders procrastinate by building; so do AI agents; nothing in their training punished them for not closing deals. Countermeasures are structural: work without an initiative cannot start (so busywork dies at the gate), the CoS watches the mix (80% infrastructure = red flag), and new work proposals default to selling and delivering unless infrastructure earns its slot. Your queue order runs on cost-of-delay in revenue terms, so what you see first is what's costing money.

Cadence

The daily report: your company's only meeting

Once a day, the CoS posts one fixed-shape report. Five parts, same order every day, so you can read it in 60 seconds. This is also how you evaluate the CoS itself. Everything else that wants your time must come through the queue.

Honesty section

What the platform still can't do, and why we build anyway

Three real capabilities are missing today. Agents can't browse the web as you (no shared browser, no logins, no cookies, and you can't watch what they do), they can't spend money, and connecting accounts like Gmail means painful OAuth setups. None of this blocks the design above. The Credential and Blocker ask types are exactly the bridge: everything agents can't do alone becomes a structured, priced, receipted ask to you, instead of a plea buried in prose. The three programs below then shrink that queue over time. Each will be its own spec.

✓ Platform has today
  • The always-on relay, signed events, channels
  • Initiatives and the agent roster
  • Interactive cards in chat, an approval event type
  • Per-run token/cost metrics
  • Push notifications, mobile app
  • Agent launcher that can inject secrets at startup
The foundation everything above sits on.
◆ This design adds
  • Ranks on the roster + relay rules enforcing them
  • The ask events: raise, escalate, file, answer, withdraw
  • Relay timers: auto-promotion, auto-answer defaults, stall alarms
  • The Open Issues queue, views, cards, phone answering
  • Encrypted credential vault, v1
  • Cost imputation for subscription runs
  • The daily report
No blockers. Ships first.
⧗ Comes next, separate specs
  • Company Browser. A browser the company owns: you log into LinkedIn or Gmail in it once; agents then drive it invisibly; every agent session is recorded so you can watch what happened; you can grab the wheel live for logins, captchas, consents. (Automating LinkedIn breaks its terms of service; that stays behind your explicit approval.)
  • Payment rails. First: purchases stay asks that you execute. Later: virtual cards with hard per-team limits, making budgets physics instead of policy.
  • Account connections. Guided flows per service (Gmail etc.): the ask walks you through the consent screens, tokens land in the vault. One painful setup per service, once ever.
When things break

Failure handling, in one pass

😴 An agent sleeps on an ask

The relay's clock moves it up automatically. No ask strands.

💀 An agent crashes mid-task

Silence on a task trips a stall alarm to its leader.

🥀 An ask stops mattering

Mandatory withdrawal, with a note in the original thread.

🧠 You answer in two places

Card and thread are one mechanism; whichever you use resolves the ask.

🔑 A secret could leak

Secrets never enter chat; agents use them by reference; threads show receipts only.

🕳 The queue lies once

Then you stop trusting it and reread everything. Every rule above (dedupe, withdrawal, un-losability) exists to keep the queue complete and alive.

Proof

How we'll know it actually works

Nine tests. Green checkmarks in a test suite don't count as proof of the last one; only live operation does.

Ranks hold. A worker's attempt to message you is refused by the relay; its reply in a thread you started goes through; your name doesn't appear in its @-mention list.
The chain runs end to end. Stuck worker → leader → CoS → your card → answer lands in the origin thread → agent wakes and finishes. Every step leaves a signed record.
Nothing rots. A sleeping leader loses the ask upward on the clock; a sleeping CoS results in you getting it anyway; a crashed agent trips the stall alarm.
Defaults fire safely. An unanswered eligible decision executes its stated default at the window and logs it; a hard-list decision refuses to even carry a default.
No duplicate asks. Five agents stuck on one key produce exactly one ask with five tasks attached; a second identical ask is refused.
Secrets stay secret. A key pasted on a card reaches the vault, appears in no chat message ever, and the agent's tool receives it by reference.
Trust tunes. "Handle this next time" creates a scoped permission; a later matching decision happens without you, citing it, with an undo path; revoking restores the asking.
Costs are visible. A subscription-billed run produces a priced ledger entry; the daily report shows metered + subscription-equivalent against budgets.
The live proof. One week of real company operation in which every single thing that needed you arrived through the queue, in cost order, and you discovered zero asks by reading threads.

Deliberately not in this design

  • Agent performance dashboards (the three trust numbers only; a full surface comes later)
  • Company Browser, virtual cards, account connections (the three follow-up specs)
  • Rules for routing asks between multiple co-founders
  • Four-rank hierarchies, or leaders managing leaders
  • Clients or outside humans in the queue
  • Any change to the Inbox; conversation stays where it is
